1. The calculation procedure

Designing a shallow foundation consists of successive, interdependent steps. This chapter details the theory of two of them — bearing resistance (bearing failure) and sliding — for both the drained and the undrained case.

The usual design workflow

  1. choosing the foundation level (bearing stratum, groundwater, frost depth)
  2. selecting the foundation type
  3. determining the foundation width — with bearing resistance (bearing failure) and settlement checks
  4. structural design of the foundation (material, depth, reinforcement)
  5. checking stability — sliding, overturning, uplift (flotation)
Structure of this chapter: the bearing resistance calculation (drained and undrained case), the treatment of the effective foundation area and the groundwater, the verification of adequacy, and finally the sliding check (drained and undrained).

2. Bearing resistance calculation

Depending on the rate of loading and the degree of water saturation, the shear strength of the soil can be accounted for in two ways — for the drained and the undrained case.

2.1 Drained case

The drained case is typical of slow loading / granular soils: the shear strength of the soil is described by the φ – c pair, and the favourable effect of water buoyancy may be taken into account. The resistance of the shallow foundation against bearing failure, according to EC7 (MSZ EN 1997-1), is:

Rk / (B′·L′) = 0.5·B′·γ′·Nγ·sγ·iγ·bγ  +  q′·Nq·sq·iq·bq  +  c·Nc·sc·ic·bc

Width (self-weight) term

0.5·B′·γ′·Nγ·sγ·iγ·bγ
comes from the foundation width and the weight of the soil beneath it

Surcharge (depth) term

q′·Nq·sq·iq·bq
the effect of the overburden pressure q′ beside the foundation base (foundation depth)

Cohesion term

c·Nc·sc·ic·bc
the resistance arising from the cohesion of the soil

Input parameters

  • γ′ — effective unit weight of the soil beneath the foundation
  • B / L — actual width / length of the foundation; B′ / L′ — dimensions of the effective foundation area
  • q′ — effective overburden (geostatic) pressure acting beside the foundation at the foundation level
  • c — cohesion of the soil beneath the foundation; φ — its angle of shearing resistance

Bearing resistance (bearing capacity) factors

Nq = eπ·tanφ · tan²(45° + φ/2)
Nγ = 2 · (Nq − 1) · tanφ
Nc = (Nq − 1) · cotφ

2.2 Undrained case

The undrained case is typical of rapid loading / fine-grained (cohesive) soils: the shear strength of the soil is described by the undrained shear strength cu, with φ = 0. Water buoyancy must not be taken into account — we work with total stresses.

σt = Rv / (B′·L′) = (2 + π)·cu·bc·sc·ic  +  q

Factors (undrained)

sc = 1 + 0.2·(B′/L′)
bc = 1 − 2α/(π + 2)
ic = 0.5·[1 + √(1 − Rh/(A′·cu))]

α must be entered in the formula in radians.

Notation

  • q — the total overburden (geostatic) pressure acting beside the foundation at the foundation level
  • cu — undrained shear strength
  • A′ = B′·L′ — the effective foundation area

3. Effective foundation area and treatment of groundwater

3.1 Effective foundation area — eccentricity

Under eccentric loading the load does not act at the centroid of the cross-section. For the calculation we use the effective foundation area, whose centroid coincides with the point of application of the resultant force:

B′ = B − 2·eB     L′ = L − 2·eL

The eccentricity follows from the moment equation written about the foundation base. If a horizontal force Hk and an eccentric load Qk act:

eB,k = | (Hk·h) − (Qk·eq) | / Vk
Watch the signs! The moments of the horizontal force and of the eccentric vertical load can act in opposite directions — in that case the eccentricity decreases.

3.2 Effect of groundwater — the effective γ′

The value of γ′ in the width term is influenced by the depth of the groundwater below the foundation base (twd). The failure zone extends to a depth of B′; γ′ varies linearly with the ratio twd/B′:

twd ≤ 0.5·B′  →  γ′ = γsat − γw   (water within the failure zone)
0.5·B′ < twd < 1.5·B′  →  γ′ = (γsat − γw) + (γm − (γsat − γw))·(twd/B′ − 0.5)   (linear transition)
1.5·B′ ≤ twd  →  γ′ = γm   (water deep, no effect)
A negative twd means the groundwater is above the foundation base — then γ′ = γsat − γw.

4. Verification of adequacy

The bearing resistance (GEO) limit state is satisfied if the design load does not exceed the design resistance:

Vd ≤ Rd

Design load — Vd

Vd = γG·Gk + γQ·Qk

Partial factors: for permanent actions γG = 1.35, for variable (imposed) actions γQ = 1.5. Gk includes the self-weight of the superstructure, the foundation, and the overlying soil (fill).

Design resistance — Rd

Rd = Rk / γR

The resistance partial factor for bearing failure of shallow foundations is γR = 1.4. The characteristic resistance Rk follows from the bearing resistance formula.

If Vd > Rd: the foundation is inadequate. There are two remedies — (1) lower the foundation level (q′ increases), or (2) widen the foundation (B increases). In practice, widening is usually the more practical option.

5. Sliding check (EQU)

If the force acting on the foundation base has a horizontal component (it is not perpendicular to the base), the safety against sliding must be checked. The condition for adequacy is:

Hd ≤ Rd + Rp;d
  • Hd — the design value of the horizontal load acting on the foundation base; it must also include the design value of the active earth pressure acting on the foundation.
  • Rd — the design value of the shearing (base) resistance mobilised at the foundation base.
  • Rp;d — the design value of the resistance arising from the (passive) earth pressure acting on the side face of the foundation.
Use Rp;d with caution! The soil in front of the face can be removed by erosion or human intervention, and in clay seasonal shrinkage can separate it from the face. The full passive earth resistance is mobilised only at large displacements, so in clay often only one-half to two-thirds of it may be relied upon.

Drained case — base friction

Rd = V′k · tan δk / γR;h
  • V′k — the characteristic value of the vertical load; only the variable load that is certainly concurrent with Hd may be included.
  • δk — the base–soil friction angle: for a cast-in-situ foundation δk = φ′k, for a precast one δk = ⅔·φ′k.
  • The cohesion c′ at the base is neglected on the safe side.
  • γR;h = 1.1 (DA-2*).

Undrained case — base adhesion

Rd = Ac · cu;k / γR;h
  • Ac — the compressed base area (B·L, or the effective A′).
  • cu;k — the characteristic undrained shear strength at the foundation base.
  • γR;h = 1.1.
  • Limit: because of the gap between base and soil (water, air), the condition Rd ≤ 0.4·Vd must also be satisfied.

1. Why calculate in two ways?

The strength of soil does not depend on the total pressure acting on it, but on how much of it is carried by the soil skeleton and how much by the water in the pores. This is Terzaghi’s principle of effective stress: σ = σ′ + u. Shear strength comes from the effective stress alone: τf = c′ + σ′·tan φ′. When a foundation is loaded, the question is whether the water “can escape” while the load is being applied — if it can, the soil skeleton takes over the load and gains strength; if not, the water takes it over and the strength of the soil does not increase.

drained “the water escapes”

The pore water drains away as fast as the load is applied, and the pore water pressure stays at its initial value (Δu ≈ 0). The load immediately becomes effective stress, and the soil gains strength as it is loaded. This is how sand and gravel always behave, and cohesive soil too after a long time, once consolidation has finished.

σ′ increases  →  τf = c′ + σ′·tan φ′ increases  →  calculation with the effective values c′, φ′, γ′ and q′

undrained “the water is trapped”

The load increases faster than the water could drain away: the volume of the saturated soil cannot change, so the water takes up the excess (Δu ≈ Δσ). The effective stress, and with it the strength, does not increase: at the moment of loading the soil is just as weak as before. This is how saturated clay and silt behave during the short period of construction.

σ′ constant  →  τf = cu (φu = 0)  →  calculation in total stresses, with cu

The “race” of rates decides

Which case applies is decided by a “race” between the rate of loading and the permeability of the soil. The time needed for consolidation depends on the permeability and on the length of the drainage path: t ∝ H² / cv. In sand this means minutes to hours, in silt weeks to months, in clay years or decades. Therefore:

Gravel, sand

Drains so quickly that it is practically always drained. Only the drained calculation is needed (c′ = 0, φ′).

Silt, silty sand

Transitional: it depends on the rate of filling. Both cases have to be calculated; the drained one often governs.

Saturated clay

Undrained during construction, drained in the long term. Under loading the undrained (short-term) case usually governs — the calculator computes both.

Why is the undrained case the dangerous one under loading? Because the soil is at its weakest while the load is being applied: the strength is still the old one, but the load is already the new one. As the water slowly seeps out, the effective stress, and with it the strength, increases — the situation can only improve. For unloading (excavation, cutting) it is the other way round: there the long-term, drained case governs, because the pore water pressure recovers over time and the soil weakens.

3. How we calculate in the two cases

Drained case — effective stresses
Rk/A′ = 0.5·γ′·B′·Nγ·sγ·iγ·bγ + q′·Nq·sq·iq·bq + c′·Nc·sc·ic·bc
  • Parameters: c′ and φ′ (effective shear strength parameters); below the groundwater the unit weight is γ′ = γsat − γw.
  • Bearing resistance factors: Nq = eπ·tan φ′·tan²(45° + φ′/2), Nc = (Nq − 1)·cot φ′, Nγ = 2·(Nq − 1)·tan φ′ — all three terms grow with the load and with the width.
  • q′: the effective overburden pressure of the soil above the foundation base; groundwater close by also reduces the γ′ term (twd/B′ rule).
  • When: sand and gravel always; silt and clay in the long term.
Undrained case — total stresses
σt = (2 + π)·cu·bc·sc·ic + q    (φu = 0 → Nc = 2 + π = 5.14)
  • Parameter: a single number, the undrained shear strength cu (from laboratory tests or penetration tests); φu = 0, so there are no Nq and Nγ terms — the bearing resistance is independent of the width, and it increases with depth only through the q term.
  • q: the total pressure of the soil above the foundation base (with γsat, without water buoyancy), because we work with total stresses.
  • Shape and inclination factors: sc = 1 + 0.2·B′/L′, ic = 0.5·[1 + √(1 − H/(A′·cu))], bc = 1 − 2α/(π + 2).
  • When: saturated clay and silt — during the short period of construction and loading.

4. A famous case: the Transcona grain elevator, 1913

What happens in the film really happened

Winnipeg, Canada

Filling of the Transcona grain elevator began in the autumn of 1913, straight after it was completed, and it was almost full within a month. Beneath the raft lay thick, soft, saturated clay. The fast filling created undrained conditions: the pore water could not escape, and the strength of the soil did not increase with the load. When the average contact pressure reached about 300 kPa, the soil was pushed out on one side and heaved on the other, and within a few hours the building tilted by some 27° — the reinforced concrete silo itself remained almost undamaged.

Decades later, the undrained bearing resistance back-calculated from cu values measured on soil samples showed good agreement with the actual failure pressure — this became one of the classic confirmations of the undrained (φu = 0) bearing resistance calculation. The silo was later straightened by underpinning and remained in use for decades.

Lesson: on saturated clay the rate of filling matters just as much as the magnitude of the load. The same silo, the same soil — filled slowly, in stages, the water would have escaped, the soil would have gained strength, and the silo would still be standing upright. The “In two stages, with a rest period” scenario shows exactly this.

The terms of the formula — where does each one come from?

The bearing resistance formula of a shallow foundation is not a “black box”: each of its terms and factors expresses a clearly visible physical effect. In this chapter we draw the failure mechanism on three-dimensional, rotatable models, and show one by one where the width, the depth and the cohesion terms come from, why the shape, load-inclination and base-inclination factors are needed, and how they change the slip surface.

Rk / (B′·L′) = 0.5·B′·γ′·Nγ·sγ·iγ·bγ  +  q′·Nq·sq·iq·bq  +  c·Nc·sc·ic·bc

Width (self-weight) term

The weight of the soil mass above the slip surface resists being pushed out. The wider the foundation, the larger (and deeper) the failure mass → proportional to B.

Surcharge (depth) term

The passive wedge also has to lift the overburden above the foundation base: its weight is q′ = γ′·D. The deeper the foundation, the larger it is.

Cohesion term

Cohesion also resists along the whole length of the slip surface — independently of friction, which is why the c term exists even when φ = 0.

1. The failure mechanism and the three terms

When the load reaches the bearing resistance, a characteristic Prandtl failure mass forms beneath the foundation: an active wedge moving downwards together with the foundation (its sides make an angle of 45° + φ/2 with the foundation base), a radial shear zone rotating around it (logarithmic spiral), and a passive wedge pushed outwards and upwards (45° − φ/2). Three things work against bearing failure: the weight of the soil mass being pushed out, the weight of the overburden above it, and the cohesion acting along the slip surface. These three are the three terms of the formula. Adjust φ: with a larger friction angle the spiral opens wider and the mass becomes deeper and wider — that is why the N factors increase so steeply.

Try φ = 0 (undrained case) — the spiral becomes a circle, Nγ = 0, the width term disappears, and only the cohesion (Nc = 5.14) and the overburden remain. Then D = 0: the depth term disappears as well.

Why 0.5·B·γ·Nγ?

Every dimension of the failure mass is proportional to B, so its volume is proportional to B² and its weight to γ·B²; per unit area of the foundation (divided by B), γ·B remains. Nγ is a multiplier that follows from the geometry (from φ), and the 0.5 is part of the traditional notation.

Why q′·Nq?

The passive wedge moves towards the surface and lifts the soil above the foundation base with it. Its weight acts on the passive wedge as a uniform surcharge (q′ = γ′·D); Nq tells how much resistance this transfers to the foundation base through the mechanism.

Why c·Nc?

At every point of the slip surface the cohesion provides shear resistance, and the length and shape of the surface depend only on φ — hence Nc = (Nq − 1)·cot φ. Nc and Nq can be derived from the same mechanism (Prandtl–Reissner); Nγ is approximate.

2. Shape factors: from strip footing to pad footing

The formula was derived for a strip footing: the failure mass is “infinite” along its length, and the soil moves only in the plane perpendicular to the foundation. For a pad footing (B × L) the mass has a finite length: slip surfaces also form on its two short sides, shear resistance develops there as well, and the soil spreads out in three dimensions. The shape factors account for this through the B/L ratio. Press the “From strip to pad footing” button, or drag the L/B slider, and watch how the end faces appear and how the factors change.

sq and sc > 1: extra shear resistance develops on the end faces, and the overburden “holds” on the short sides too — the depth and cohesion terms increase. sγ < 1: the three-dimensional failure mass is relatively smaller, so less soil weight acts per unit area of the foundation — the width term decreases (an experimental result, De Beer). For a square foundation: sγ = 0.7, sq = 1 + sin φ.

The factors always contain the effective B′/L′, and B is the side in whose direction the horizontal force acts (the convention of the worked examples). For a strip footing B/L → 0, and all shape factors are 1.

3. Load inclination: why does the bearing resistance decrease?

If the resultant force is not perpendicular to the foundation base, the horizontal component pushes the soil in the direction of H: the mechanism becomes one-sided and flatter, the slip surface moves a smaller, shallower soil mass, and part of the friction angle is already “used up” by shear along the foundation base. Less soil resists → lower bearing resistance. This is what the i factors express: they are all less than 1, and they decrease rapidly as the H/V ratio grows. If δ reached φ, the result would no longer be bearing failure but sliding — that is the task of the EQU check; this is why the slider allows H/V only up to 0.9·tan φ, so that you always see the bearing failure mechanism.

iγ = (1 − f)m+1, iq = (1 − f)m, ic = (iq·Nq − 1)/(Nq − 1), where f = H/(V + A′·c·cot φ), and m = 2 for a strip footing. Cohesion “helps” in the denominator: the same H gives a smaller f on cohesive soil. On the model the flattening of the slip surface is illustrative (a mechanism calculated with φ − δ); the factors are exact.

Why is it a reduction factor? Under a vertical load the full φ works against bearing failure. Under an inclined load part of the shearing already starts at the foundation base (as in sliding), and the failure mass is smaller and asymmetric — the resistance that can be mobilised is smaller. The width term is the most sensitive (exponent m + 1), because the soil mass being pushed out shrinks the most.

4. The depth term: the weight of the overburden

The soil above the foundation base, the overburden, helps in two ways: its weight presses as a uniform surcharge on the passive wedge, which the failure has to lift, and its shear strength also resists the movement. The EC7 formula takes only the first into account: q′ = γ′·D (the shear strength of the overburden is neglected on the safe side, which is why there is no separate depth factor “d”). Drag the D slider: the overburden gets thicker, the q′·Nq term increases linearly, and the passive wedge has to push out an ever larger mass. With groundwater the overburden is “lighter” (γ′ = γ − γw), and the term is smaller.

In practice, therefore, one of the most effective tools is to lower the foundation level: not only does q′ increase, but the foundation often reaches a better (deeper) bearing stratum as well. The q′ term of the formula takes only the weight of the soil beside the foundation; the weight of the foundation itself and of the fill on top of it appears on the load side.

5. The N factors

All three bearing resistance factors depend solely on φ and grow exponentially with φ — because of the logarithmic spiral. The chart shows them on a decimal (linear) scale: below 25° the curves are flat, above it they “take off” — from 30° to 40°, Nγ more than triples. This is why φ should be determined carefully, and why it must not be “rounded”.

Nq = eπ·tan φ·tan²(45° + φ/2)
Nc = (Nq − 1)·cot φ
Nγ = 2·(Nq − 1)·tan φ

The eπ·tan φ comes from the π/2 rotation of the spiral (r = r0·eθ·tan φ), and tan²(45° + φ/2) from the stress ratio of the active and passive Rankine wedges.

6. Inclination of the foundation base: the b factors

If the foundation base is inclined at an angle α, one edge of the foundation lies higher and the other lower relative to the horizontal ground surface. Failure starts towards the higher edge: there the ground “falls away” relative to the foundation base, so the spiral of the radial zone rotates only through π/2 − α, the slip surface is shorter and the passive wedge smaller — less soil resists, and the resistance that can be mobilised decreases. The b factors, which are less than 1, account for this. Drag the α slider: the inclined foundation moves an ever smaller mechanism (the symmetric mechanism of a horizontal base is shown dashed for comparison), and the bar chart on the right shows how much bearing resistance is lost.

bq = bγ = (1 − α·tan φ)², bc = bq − (1 − bq)/(Nc·tan φ), with α in radians (EC7 Annex D). On the model B = 2 m, D = 1 m; the foundation settles perpendicular to its base, and the mechanism is one-sided, towards the higher edge. For a small inclination (5°) the reduction is a few per cent; at 15° it is already 20–30%.

Vertical load on an inclined base: V also has a component parallel to the foundation base (H = V·sin α), which the load-inclination factors i account for — the b factors additionally express the effect of the inclined plane itself. In the calculation V and H always mean the components perpendicular and parallel to the foundation base (see WE7).
In summary: the three terms of the formula are the three resisting effects (soil weight, overburden, cohesion); the N factors account for the geometry of the mechanism, the s factors for its three-dimensional nature, the i factors for the mechanism shrinking under an inclined load, and the b factors for the inclined foundation base. Task

Check the adequacy of the pad footing (B = 0.95 m, L = 1.45 m) shown in the figure against horizontal sliding (EQU, drained). h = 0.95 m; t = 2.05 m; tfill = 3.0 m; γb = 25 kN/m³. Loads: Gz,k = 490 kN (permanent) and Qz,k = 610 kN (variable) vertical, plus Qx,k = 250 kN horizontal; eb = 0.04 m. The vertical loads include the weight of the foundation and of the soil lying above it. The groundwater is 0.95 m above the foundation base (hGWL). sand fill: γ = 19.5 kN/m³, γsat = 20.5 kN/m³, φ = 32°, c = 0 kPa; silt: γ = 19.5 kN/m³, γsat = 20.5 kN/m³, φ = 16°, c = 40 kPa. Between the foundation and the soil, δ = ⅔·φ is to be used.

Solution

1. Governing horizontal load — active earth pressure + column load

Horizontal column load: Qx,k = 250 kN.   Coefficient of the active earth pressure acting on the foundation (sand fill): Ka = tan²(45°−φ/2) = tan²(45°−16°) = 0.307

The vertical geostatic stress over the height of the foundation (groundwater at the top of the foundation): σ′v,top = 19.5·2.05 = 39.98 kPa;   σ′v,bot = 39.98 + (20.5−10)·0.95 = 49.95 kPa

Horizontal active stress: σ′h = Ka·σ′v → 12.27 and 15.34 kPa.   Resultant: Ea,k = (12.27+15.34)/2·h·L = 13.80·0.95·1.45 = 19 kN

Hd = γG·Ea,k + γQ·Qx,k = 1.35·19 + 1.5·250 = 400.65 kN

2. Base friction resistance

Only the permanent vertical load contributes: V′k = Gz,k = 490 kN (on the safe side, the variable Qz,k and the cohesion at the base are neglected).

Precast foundation → δk = ⅔·φ = ⅔·16° = 10.67° → tan δk = 0.189;   γR;h = 1.1

Rd = V′k·tan δk / γR;h = (490·0.189)/1.1 = 84.2 kN

3. Passive earth resistance on the back face of the foundation

Kp = tan²(45°+φ/2) = tan²(45°+16°) = 3.255.   The passive stress: σ′h,p = Kp·σ′v → 130.12 and 162.59 kPa.

Ep,k = (130.12+162.59)/2·h·L = 146.35·0.95·1.45 = 201.6 kN  →  Rpd = Ep,k/γR;h = 201.6/1.1 = 183.27 kN

4. Sliding check

Rd + Rpd = 84.2 + 183.27 = 267.47 kN < Hd = 400.65 kN

Result: Rd + Rpd = 267.47 kN < Hd = 400.65 kN → NOT ADEQUATE. ✗   Because of the large horizontal load, the base friction and the full passive earth resistance together are still not enough; a larger foundation width or deeper embedment is needed.

Calculator

One calculator for the complete check of a shallow foundation: any number of soil layers, strip or pad footing, concentric and eccentric loads (for a pad footing even in two directions), horizontal forces in both directions, an inclined foundation base and groundwater. The program computes the drained and the undrained bearing resistance and the sliding check at the same time, derived step by step — the model can be rotated in 3D, and the data of the worked examples can be loaded with one click.

Calculation conventions

Loads and self-weight

  • The loads are characteristic values at the top of the foundation: Gk permanent and Qk variable vertical load, HB,k horizontal force parallel to side B and HL,k parallel to side L (the latter for a pad footing only). The nature of H can be chosen: permanent (γG) or variable (γQ) — the same for both components. For a strip footing all values refer to a 1 m long section, in kN/m.
  • When self-weight is switched on, the foundation (Gb,k = B·L·h·γb, γb = 25 kN/m³) and the fill above it (Gf,k = (B·L − b·l)·Σγi·ti over the thickness D − h, with the moist unit weight, neglecting buoyancy) are added to Gk as permanent loads — as in the practical material.
  • Design values: Vd = γG·Gk + γQ·Qk; the horizontal load with its resultant: Hd = γH·√(HB,k² + HL,k²). Default values: γG = 1.35, γQ = 1.5, γR = 1.4 (bearing failure), γR;h = 1.1 (sliding) — all of them can be edited.

Input ranges and interdependent limits

  • Permitted ranges of the dimensions: B = 0.5–5 m, L = 0.5–5 m, D = 0.5–4 m, h = 0.5–4 m, b = 0.2–1.0 m. The sliders move within these ranges, and a typed value is clipped to the edge of the range.
  • The dimensions limit one another, so that no meaningless foundation can be entered: the footing height cannot exceed the depth of the foundation base (h ≤ D — if D is reduced, h decreases with it); the wall or column cannot overhang the foundation (b ≤ B, and for a pad footing b ≤ L as well); the eccentricity of Qk cannot exceed half the foundation width (eQ ≤ B/2, eQ,L ≤ L/2), i.e. the load cannot fall outside the foundation. The limit of a dependent dimension is shown below its field and adjusts immediately when the controlling dimension changes.
  • The scale of the 3D model and of the 2D section adapts to the dimensions for every setting: the width and depth of the soil block grow with B, L, D, the layer thicknesses and the groundwater; for a large foundation the spacing of arrows and labels increases proportionally, and in the 2D section the foundation occupies at most the middle band of the drawing, so the layer and dimension labels always fit.

Eccentricity — in two directions

  • The horizontal forces act at the top of the foundation (their lever arm about the foundation base is h), and Qk acts with the eccentricities eQ (B direction) and eQ,L (L direction). From the moment equation, for each direction: eB = |HB,k·h ∓ Qk·eQ| / Vk and eL = |HL,k·h ∓ Qk·eQ,L| / Vk, where Vk = Gk + Qk; the ∓ sign is set by the switch (moments in opposite or in the same direction). In the figures (3D and 2D) the H arrow is shown on the top face of the foundation and Q on the side corresponding to its eccentricity; on the foundation base the point of application of the resultant and the effective area B′ (for a pad footing B′ × L′) are marked in teal, and the resultant always shifts in the direction of H.
  • Effective area: B′ = B − 2·eB, L′ = L − 2·eL, A′ = B′·L′. For a strip footing L′ = 1 m and eL = 0. If eB > B/3 or eL > L/3, the effective area model is no longer valid — the program gives a warning, and the size of the foundation or the arrangement of the loads has to be changed.

Bearing resistance (GEO)

  • The bearing resistance is calculated with the shear strength of the layer below the foundation base (φ, c, or cu), without averaging. The effective overburden pressure q′ (the total q in the undrained case) follows from the whole layer sequence and the groundwater: γ above the water, γsat − γw below it (γsat for total stress). γ′ depends on the ratio twd/B′: ≤ 0.5 → γsat − γw; ≥ 1.5 → γ; linear transition in between.
  • Bearing resistance factors: Nq = eπ·tanφ·tan²(45° + φ/2), Nc = (Nq − 1)·cotφ, Nγ = 2·(Nq − 1)·tanφ. Shape factors for a pad footing with the ratio B′/L′ (sγ = 1 − 0.3·B′/L′, sq = 1 + (B′/L′)·sinφ, sc = (sq·Nq − 1)/(Nq − 1)), where B is the direction of HB — the convention of the worked examples; for a strip footing s = 1.
  • Load inclination with the resultant of H, using characteristic values: f = H/(Vk + A′·c·cotφ). The exponent depends on the direction of H (EC7 Annex D): mB = (2 + B′/L′)/(1 + B′/L′), mL = (2 + L′/B′)/(1 + L′/B′), m = mL·cos²θ + mB·sin²θ, where cosθ = HL/H and sinθ = HB/H (m = 2 for a strip footing). iγ = (1 − f)m+1, iq = (1 − f)m, ic = (iq·Nq − 1)/(Nq − 1).
  • Inclination of the foundation base (α): bq = bγ = (1 − α·tanφ)², bc = bq − (1 − bq)/(Nc·tanφ), with α in radians.
  • Drained: Rk/A′ = 0.5·γ′·B′·Nγ·sγ·iγ·bγ + q′·Nq·sq·iq·bq + c·Nc·sc·ic·bc; Rd = Rk/γR. Undrained (if the layer below the base has a cu): σt = (2 + π)·cu·bc·sc·ic + q, where sc = 1 + 0.2·B′/L′, bc = 1 − 2α/(π + 2), ic = 0.5·[1 + √(1 − H/(A′·cu))]; Rd = σt·A′/γR. Adequate if Vd ≤ Rd.

Sliding (EQU)

  • The active earth pressure acts on the front face of the foundation, of height h and width L (1 m for a strip footing); it is obtained with effective stresses, layer by layer, with the coefficient Ka = tan²(45° − φ/2) (the water pressure is balanced on the two sides). When switched on, it is added to Hd as a permanent load: Hd = γH·H + γG·Ea,k, where H is the resultant of the horizontal forces — on the safe side, the earth pressure is taken into account at full value, regardless of direction.
  • Base friction is provided only by the permanent vertical load (V′k = Gk including self-weight), and cohesion is neglected: Rd = V′k·tanδ / γR;h, δ = φ (cast in situ) or ⅔·φ (precast), with the φ of the layer below the base.
  • Earth resistance on the resisting side can be set to none, at rest (K0 = 1 − sinφ) or passive (Kp = tan²(45° + φ/2)), on the same face; Rp;d = η·Ek/γR;h, where η is the mobilised fraction (the full passive resistance is mobilised only at large displacements). Adequate if Hd ≤ Rd + Rp;d.
  • Undrained alternative: Rd = A′·cu/γR;h, and the limit Rd ≤ 0.4·Vd must also be satisfied — the program takes the smaller value.

The 3D model and the 2D section

  • The soil block is transparent, with the foundation and the wall/column solid inside it. For a strip footing the wall and the foundation continue along the length, and the calculation refers to the highlighted 1 m long section. The arrows show the characteristic loads (Vk = Gk + Qk from the superstructure and the self-weight, Q with its eccentricity, HB,k and HL,k), and the labels show the parameters of the layers and the design groundwater.
  • The 2D section button shows the same state in a vertical section: layers, groundwater, foundation, effective width (B′), loads; the 3D model can be rotated, the 2D section can be read at a glance.
  • Below the figure the checks are listed one under the other: drained bearing resistance, undrained bearing resistance, sliding; each row compares resistance and load and shows the verdict and a utilisation bar. Clicking a row opens its derivation; the design load, the eccentricity and the effective area appear in steps 1–3 of the derivation.

The drained–undrained film (3D silo)

  • Illustrative model: the load of the silo acts on a B × B slab at a depth of D = 1 m, with the groundwater at ground level. The load q(t) increases linearly to qmax during the filling time (in two-stage mode in two steps, with a rest period between them whose length is twice the consolidation time of the soil, at most 2 years).
  • Settlement in the film: s = (0.12·q + 0.88·Δσ′)·1.2·B/(2·Eoed) — a small immediate (distortional) part and a consolidation part proportional to the effective stress; Eoed = 4 MPa for clay, 8 MPa for silt and 30 MPa for sand. In the 3D scene the settlement is shown magnified 3 times, and the original foundation base is marked with a dashed line; the saturation of the blue zone beneath the base is proportional to the excess pore water pressure, and the water column of the piezometer shows Δu, scaled to qmax.

The 3D models of “The terms of the formula” chapter

  • Failure mechanism: a Prandtl mass for a strip footing — an active wedge at 45° + φ/2, a logarithmic spiral r = r0·eθ·tan φ around the edge of the foundation with a rotation of π/2, and a passive Rankine wedge at 45° − φ/2; the passive slip surface continues at the same angle through the overburden up to ground level (the q′ term of the formula replaces the overburden with a distributed load acting at the level of the foundation base, while the drawing continues the slip surface up to the ground surface, so as the overburden increases, the run-out of the mechanism and the heaving block grow); the depth of the mass depends on φ and B, and its run-out on D as well. The movement is an illustrative, exaggerated animation, but it follows the kinematics of the mechanism: the active wedge settles together with the foundation; the radial zone stays on the slip surface, and inside it the soil “flows” by rotating about the edge of the foundation (E) (downwards beside the wedge, upwards and outwards towards the passive wedge); the passive wedge and the overburden above it slide out together, upwards and outwards in the direction of the slip surface (45° − φ/2), with a displacement proportional to the velocity that increases along the spiral (by a factor of eπ/2·tan φ); above the passive zone the ground heaves up to where the slip surface emerges (the heave is full at the emergence point, small at the wall of the foundation and convex in between; where the slip surface emerges, a step against the stationary ground shows the outward sliding); the slip surface stays in place. The arrows show the direction of movement, and their length adapts to the size of the mechanism; in the depth explorer the thin brown arrows drawn on the ground surface indicate the weight of the overburden (q′), not displacement.
  • The three terms and every factor are calculated with the calculator’s formulas: 0.5·γ·B·Nγ·sγ·iγ·bγ, q′·Nq·sq·iq·bq, c·Nc·sc·ic·bc; D = 0.5 m is fixed in the shape explorer, D = 0.8 m in the inclination explorer and B = 2 m in the depth explorer. The mechanism for an inclined load is one-sided in the direction of H, drawn with an angle of φ − 0.9·δ and a shifted active wedge (illustration), and the i factors are exact; the ratio H/V can be at most 0.9·tan φ (the slider range narrows with φ), so the slip surface of bearing failure is always shown. Every 3D model (including the silo film) can be switched to a 2D section: this is a vertical section of the same scene through the axis of the foundation (a front view without rotation), with the same displacements and labels.
  • Inclination of the foundation base (explorer 6): B = 2 m and D = 1 m are fixed, α is at most 15°; the mechanism is drawn towards the higher edge, because there the ground “falls away” by α relative to the foundation base: the spiral of the radial zone rotates only through π/2 − α, the passive wedge runs up to the horizontal plane at the level of the higher edge, and the slip surface up to the ground surface; the foundation settles perpendicular to its base, and the active wedge moves with it. bq = bγ = (1 − α·tan φ)², bc = bq − (1 − bq)/(Nc·tan φ), with α in radians; the bar chart shows the terms multiplied by b, relative to the value for a horizontal base. V is drawn vertical; its component parallel to the foundation base (V·sin α) belongs to the i factors, and the model shows only the effect of b.
  • The chart of the N factors uses a decimal (linear) scale from 0 to 300, for φ = 0–45° (values above 300, Nγ near 45°, fall outside the chart).
  • The 3D scenes (explorers, silo film, calculator) rotate about the centre of the soil block; the scale adapts to the horizontal diagonal of the block and to its largest vertical extent, so the whole model stays on the canvas at any rotation angle (the default zoom of the calculator is 1.0, and it can be zoomed by scrolling). The labels stay within the edge of the canvas.
  • The proportion of the end faces of a pad footing approximates the base area (2·Asection/(B·L)); the shape factors are those of EC7 Annex D, and for φ = 0, sc = 1 + 0.2·B/L.
  • Pore water pressure: every increment of load first goes into the pore water (saturated soil, Δu = Δσ) and seeps away with a time constant Tc characteristic of the soil (dΔu/dt = dq/dt − Δu/Tc); Tc = 2 years for clay, 1 month for silt and half a day for sand. The excess effective stress is Δσ′ = q − Δu. This is a first-order approximation, simpler than real consolidation.
  • Undrained strength: cu = cu,0 + α·Δσ′ (strength increasing with consolidation; clay cu,0 = 30 kPa, α = 0.25; silt 45 kPa, 0.30). Undrained bearing resistance for a strip footing: (2 + π)·cu + γsat·D; drained: c′·Nc + γ′·D·Nq + 0.5·γ′·B·Nγ (without shape and inclination factors). The smaller of the two governs; for sand only the drained one is meaningful.
  • Bearing failure occurs when the load reaches the current governing bearing resistance (without partial factors, showing the actual failure); the film then stops, the silo tilts by 24°, the soil heaves on the −X side and the slip surface appears. The drawing rules of the 3D scene are the same as those of the calculator’s 3D model (transparent block, painter’s algorithm).
